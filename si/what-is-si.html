What is Superintelligence (SI)? The official name, explained

On September 29, 2026, the President of the United States signed an executive order directing federal executive-branch agencies to say “Super Intelligence” (SI) where they used to say “Artificial Intelligence” (AI). Here is what the order says, when it became official, what it changes, and what it does not.

The short answer

Superintelligence (SI) is the word US executive-branch agencies are now directed to use where they used to write artificial intelligence (AI). That is a rule about vocabulary. In the order itself, SI is defined by pointing at the existing legal definition of AI, so for the purposes of the order the two terms cover the same technologies.[1][4]

This matters because “superintelligence” also has an older meaning in research and public debate: an intellect far beyond the best human minds.[6] The order does not use that meaning. The guide sets the two side by side.

When did it become official?

The order is dated and signed September 29, 2026. The White House posted the text and a fact sheet under that date.[2][3] It was published in the Federal Register on October 2, 2026.[1] The order contains no separate effective-date clause, and the Federal Register lists none, so we date it from signing: September 29, 2026.

What the order says

The order has four short sections. Here they are, with the key words quoted from the text.[1]

1. The policy

The opening section says the terminology used by the Federal Government “should reflect the transformative capabilities of these technologies” and sets the rule, to the maximum extent permitted by law:

“the executive branch shall use the terms ‘Super Intelligence’ and ‘SI’ in place of ‘Artificial Intelligence’ and ‘AI’”Executive Order 14434, Section 1

Its stated reasoning is that today’s systems “do much more than imitate or automate discrete aspects of human intelligence” and that “they increasingly represent not merely artificial intelligence, but a new era of Super Intelligence.”[1]

2. Where agencies must use it

“in official correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory documents within the executive branch”Executive Order 14434, Section 2(a)

The same section adds a limit: Nothing in this section requires the alteration of previously issued regulations, Presidential actions, contracts, grants, or other historical documents.[1]

3. What the term means

“mean the technologies and systems encompassed by the term ‘artificial intelligence’ as defined in section 9401(3) of title 15, United States Code”Executive Order 14434, Section 3(a)

That statute defines artificial intelligence as a machine-based system that can make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments.[4] The order says this definition governs “unless and until superseded” by later Presidential action or an Act of Congress.[1]

4. What happens next

Within 60 days, the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology is to send the President proposed legislative language for a federal definition of SI. The proposal must assess whether, and how far, the new definition should modify, expand or replace the existing statutory one, suggest conforming changes to laws that mention artificial intelligence, and recommend any further executive action.[1]

The order also says it is to be carried out consistent with applicable law, does not change any agency’s legal authority, and creates no rights that anyone can enforce against the United States.[1]

What it means in practice

  • For federal agencies: websites, reports, press releases and policy documents are directed to say “Super Intelligence” or “SI” where they would have said “artificial intelligence” or “AI”.[1]
  • For existing law: statutes, regulations, contracts and grants already on the books still say “artificial intelligence”, and the order does not require them to change. Both terms will sit side by side in the federal record.[1]
  • For everyone else: the operative text addresses executive departments and agencies. It does not mention private companies, state governments, Congress, the courts, or other countries.[1]

What it does not say

Read this part twice
  • It does not say any machine is smarter than people. It sets no capability threshold. Its opening section describes today’s systems as increasingly representing “a new era”, which is a description of direction. Nowhere does the text say a system has surpassed human intelligence.[1]
  • It does not change any law, regulation, contract or grant.[1]
  • It does not bind private companies or the public.[1]
  • It does not yet create a new federal definition. It orders a proposal for one.[1]
  • It creates no legal right or benefit.[1]

Two meanings of “superintelligence”

The same word now carries two meanings. Keeping them apart is the single most useful habit when you read about this topic.

Where the name came from

The order’s opening recalls that “American scientists first gave the field its name.”[1] The phrase “artificial intelligence” appears in the title of a proposal dated August 31, 1955, by four researchers, for a two-month summer study at Dartmouth College in 1956.[5] The word “superintelligence” in its research sense has its own literature; one frequently cited definition comes from a 1997 paper, revised in 1998.[6]

How to read a headline that says SI

  1. Which meaning is it? The federal naming rule, or the research idea of an intellect far beyond ours?
  2. Is it a fact, a forecast or a claim? The order is a fact: you can read it. A prediction about what machines will do is not.
  3. Who says so, and where can you check? Follow the link to the primary text, as we do on this page.
