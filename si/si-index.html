How coverage of SI grew

The headline number

In the 47 days before the order was signed (August 13 to September 28), about 7 headlines a day used the word “superintelligence” or “super intelligence”. In the five days from signing (September 29 to October 3) it was more than 50 a day, over seven times as many. The peak was over 120 in a single day, on September 30.[1][2]

Because the number of articles we read varies from day to day, the fairer comparison is a share: headlines using the word per 10,000 articles read. Over the 47 days before the order the share was about 6 per 10,000. Since signing it has been about 39 per 10,000, almost six times higher.

After the peak the count eased: about 30 headlines on October 2 and about 10 on October 3.

SI, AGI and AI in headlines

Superintelligence is still a small share of the conversation. In the week of the order, about one headline in three (more than 30%) still used “AI” or “artificial intelligence”. About one in 340 used “superintelligence”.[2]

Against AGI, the picture has turned. In the week of the order, headlines using SI outnumbered headlines using AGI by more than five to one.

What the world is talking about

Our reading is organised into 22 categories. The largest by volume is agents (agentic AI), with over 100,000 articles, followed by news about the major technology companies, then policy, funding, and China and world AI. About 26% of articles are not yet assigned to a category.

How much is rumor

We tag articles by story type. Among about 89,000 tagged articles (snapshot of September 27, 2026), about one in 60 is a rumor.[4] Rumor is a small slice. Most tagged articles are software and model releases, research, or have no single type.

An early sample

Our hourly bulletin labels every story Confirmed, Reported or Still open. In its first six bulletins it featured 27 distinct stories: 24 Reported, 2 Confirmed and 1 Still open. That is a launch-day sample, so we read no rate into it. It does show a useful habit: most news is reported, not confirmed.

How to read these numbers

  • They are headlines in our reading. A large sample of the news, not the whole web and not a poll of what people think.
  • They are rounded down so they never overstate, and they carry an as-of date.
  • The rise follows the order, but we cannot say how much of it is the order. A large share of the headlines since September 29 are about the order itself; other news mentioning the word is counted too.
  • Shares beat raw counts. We read in bursts, so we compare per 10,000 articles read when looking across weeks.
  • Tags are not verdicts. A rumor tag says how a story is told, not that it is false.
