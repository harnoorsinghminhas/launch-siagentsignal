Where SI goes next: our outlook

Analysis, not reporting

This document is our outlook. It is clearly separate from the facts in the other documents of the library. The scenarios below are not predictions, they are not ranked, and we assign them no probabilities. They can overlap, and they are not the only possibilities. We take no position for or against the order.

What we know

These are facts, with sources. The rest of this page is analysis built on them.

  • The President of the United States signed Executive Order 14434 on September 29, 2026. It was published in the Federal Register on October 2, 2026.[1][2]
  • It directs executive departments and agencies to use “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in place of “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI”, to the maximum extent permitted by law.[1]
  • It defines SI, for the purposes of the order, as the technologies and systems covered by the existing statutory definition of artificial intelligence, 15 U.S.C. 9401(3).[1][3]
  • Within 60 days, the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology is to send the President proposed legislative language for a federal definition. By our count that is November 28, 2026. The order does not say the proposal will be made public.[1]
  • In our newsroom’s reading, headlines using the word “superintelligence” ran at more than seven times the earlier daily rate in the five days after signing, and have eased since the peak.[4]

For the full account of what the order says and does not say, see Document 1.

The open questions

  1. Will the legal definition change? The order asks for an assessment of whether the new definition should “modify, expand upon, or otherwise supersede” the existing statutory one.[1] It leaves the answer open.
  2. Will anyone outside the executive branch adopt the term? The operative text addresses federal agencies only.[1] What companies, researchers, other governments and news organisations do is not something the order decides.
  3. Will the word keep two meanings? Today “superintelligence” means one thing in the order and another in research debate. See the guide.

Three scenarios

Each scenario says what it would look like, what would point toward it, and what it would mean for a reader.

Scenario 1

A style rule

What it looks like. SI stays a naming convention inside federal agencies. Statutes, technical standards and most writing outside government keep saying “AI”. The two terms coexist without much friction.

What would point toward it. The 60-day proposal does not lead to a bill or a change in the statutory definition. Agency documents mix the terms. The text of 15 U.S.C. 9401(3) stays as it is.[3]

What it would mean for you. Search for both terms when you look for federal material. Treat “SI” in a federal document as “AI”.

Scenario 2

A new definition

What it looks like. The proposal leads to a federal definition of SI that differs from the existing definition of AI, so SI acquires its own legal scope.

What would point toward it. The proposed legislative language becomes public. A bill that defines SI, or amends the AI definition, is introduced.[5] Agencies begin to cite the new definition rather than the old one.

What it would mean for you. Rules could attach to the term, so the exact wording of the definition would matter more than the name. Read the definition text first, and ask whether it sets a capability bar.

Scenario 3

Two vocabularies

What it looks like. “Superintelligence” becomes common in public and business language while “AI” remains the word in technical, legal and international settings. The federal meaning and the research meaning live side by side, and ambiguity grows.

What would point toward it. In our SI Index, the share of headlines using the word stays well above its pre-order level for months, rather than settling back.[4] Outside organisations adopt the term in some settings and not others.

What it would mean for you. Always ask which meaning is in use. The 60-second test is built for this.

What would change our view

  • A published proposal or bill with a definition that sets a capability bar, which would make the research meaning part of the federal meaning.
  • A change in how federal agencies actually write, in either direction.
  • A sustained fall, or a sustained rise, in how often headlines use the word.
